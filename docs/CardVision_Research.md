# CardVision Research Report: Optical Object-Matching for Trading Card Verification

**Scope note.** This report treats CardVision as a **1:1 biometric-style verification problem** — "is scan B the same physical card as reference scan A" — not a classification/authentication-of-genuineness problem, and not a 1:N search/identification problem. That framing (verification vs. identification) is itself a standard distinction in the biometrics literature, e.g. NIST's evaluation programs treat fingerprint/face "verification" (1:1 template match against a claimed identity) and "identification" (1:N search) as different tasks with different error-rate semantics.[^nist-fpvte] CardVision is the former: no database search, one enrolled reference, one probe, accept/reject.

---

## 1. The honest answer on "guarantee"

**Verdict: no photo-based system — CardVision's or anyone else's, including the vendors in §2 — can honestly claim a "guarantee" of same-object matching. The correct frame is a calibrated False Accept Rate (FAR) / False Reject Rate (FRR) operating point, and the achievable FAR/FRR for a phone-camera trading card is meaningfully worse than the marketing claims of the closest commercial analogue (Alitheon), and worse than dedicated-sensor biometrics.**

The vocabulary matters and is not interchangeable:
- **FAR** (also called False Match Rate) — probability the system says "same object" when it's actually two different objects. This is the number that matters for the return-swap fraud vector: a buyer swaps in a worse copy and the system must not accept it as the original.
- **FRR** (False Non-Match Rate) — probability the system says "different object" when it actually is the same card, just photographed differently (different lighting, angle, or after legitimate shelf/shipping wear). This is the number that matters for false-flagging honest sellers/buyers.
- **EER** — the threshold at which FAR = FRR; a single-number summary of separability, lower is better, but a deployed system should not actually run at its EER — it should pick a threshold based on which error is more costly (here, FAR is more costly than FRR, because FAR silently lets fraud through while FRR only causes an annoying "inconclusive, please resubmit" support case).[^signzy][^sciencedirect-crossover]

There is no independently published, peer-reviewed FAR/FRR curve for optical trading-card matching from phone photos. What exists is (a) vendor marketing claims with no disclosed methodology (Alitheon), (b) an academic literature on the *general* problem of texture/microstructure-based object fingerprinting (paper, banknotes) using **dedicated, controlled-illumination scanners** — not phone cameras, and (c) the biometrics literature's own experience with what happens to error rates when you take a *high-information-content, well-studied* biometric (the fingerprint) and move from a purpose-built contact sensor to a phone camera under ambient conditions. That last data point is the most honest analogue available and it is not encouraging:

- Dedicated fingerprint sensors, under NIST's own long-running Fingerprint Vendor Technology Evaluation (FpVTE) program, are benchmarked to error rates far below 1% under controlled enrollment.[^nist-fpvte]
- The moment you swap in a **phone camera** for the same finger-ridge biometric (a "fingerphoto"), reported EERs jump to roughly **6%–15%** even in dedicated fingerphoto-recognition research using models built specifically for this problem.[^fingerphoto] A cross-sensor contactless-vs-contact match study reported an EER of **10.64%**.[^ridgebase]

A trading card is a *harder* surface to fingerprint than a fingertip for three structural reasons, none of which are solved by better software alone:
1. **Information density.** A fingerprint is a dense, high-contrast ridge pattern covering the whole capture area by design. A card's exploitable "fingerprint" is incidental print-defect/micro-scratch texture, often concentrated in a few print-mottled regions and largely absent on large flat color fields — this is exactly what CardVision's own `detail_score` heuristic in `residual.py`/`score.py` is trying to detect and gate on (see §3).
2. **Surface area.** The academic paper-fiber PUF literature is explicit that its confidence comes from *a lot of surface*: Buchanan et al. 2005 (the foundational Nature paper) and the Clarkson et al. 2009 paper-scanner work both rely on **several square centimeters of raw, uncoated paper fiber texture**, scanned from **multiple angles with a dedicated laser/flatbed scanner** to reconstruct a 3-D height map — not a single 2-D phone photo.[^nature2005][^clarkson2009] A trading card offers a smaller total area, and a meaningful fraction of that area (foil, gloss coating, printed art) is not raw paper fiber at all — see foil discussion below.
3. **Coating/surface treatment.** Cardstock trading cards are frequently laminated/glossy (see e.g. Pokémon full-art/textured cards) specifically to *change* the paper surface, which is good for anti-counterfeiting via *deliberately engineered* microtexture but bad for exploiting *naturally occurring* fiber randomness the way the paper-PUF literature does — the surface CardVision is fingerprinting is partly manufactured, not purely random, and that manufactured texture is shared across every copy of the same print run, not unique per physical card. This is inference, not a cited finding: no paper directly tests foil-coated trading cardstock, but it follows directly from the paper-PUF literature's own stated mechanism (random fiber orientation under the surface) not applying to a laminated/foil layer sitting on top of it.

**What this means concretely for CardVision:** treat "match" as a probabilistic accept decision at a chosen threshold, not proof. Publish (internally, and eventually to users) a stated operating point — e.g., "at our current threshold, an independent test set showed X% FAR, Y% FRR" — the moment real labeled data exists (see §4), and never use the word "guarantee," "verified," or "proof" in user-facing copy for a `match` decision. "Verified" is a *particularly* dangerous word to use for the return-swap scenario specifically: if AuctionHous tells a buyer "verified match" and it's later shown to be a false accept on a swapped card, that's the platform's own tooling providing cover for the fraud it was built to catch — worse than having no tool. The existing `insufficient_detail` decision-ladder rung (§3, `score.py`) is the single most important piece of the whole pipeline from a "don't overclaim" standpoint, because it is CardVision's version of a biometric system's **Failure-to-Acquire (FTA)** gate — refusing to render a confident decision when the input quality can't support one, rather than quietly returning a low-confidence "match" that reads the same as a high-confidence one.[^fta]

## 2. Technology landscape

### 2.1 Alitheon FeaturePrint — the closest real-world analogue, and explicitly claims trading cards

**What they claim.** Alitheon markets FeaturePrint as "Biometrics for Things": a photo (phone or standard camera) of an object's surface — "natural texture, grain, and irregularities present in every manufactured item" — is converted into a compact digital identity, with **no tags, marks, or modifications** to the object.[^alitheon-about] Their own marketing states **>99.9% accuracy, ~0.1% false negative rate, and a false-positive (FAR) rate cited as low as 10⁻⁹**, and separately ">99.99% accuracy with zero false positives" in other materials.[^alitheon-accuracy] They explicitly list trading cards/collectibles/sports cards (citing Pokémon and Fanatics-era sports cards by name) as a use case, framed around **no-touch serialization** (avoiding physical grading-slab damage) and **return-fraud prevention** — i.e., almost exactly AuctionHous's fraud vector #2.[^alitheon-collectibles]

**How much of this is independently verifiable — very little.** No third-party, peer-reviewed evaluation of FeaturePrint's FAR/FRR exists in the public literature; the accuracy figures above come only from Alitheon's own PR and conference-slide materials (e.g. a Hannover Messe presentation and press releases), not a disclosed test protocol, dataset, or methodology comparable to how NIST publishes FRVT/FpVTE results.[^alitheon-hannover] Treat the 10⁻⁹ FAR figure as a marketing claim, not a validated fact — it is two to three orders of magnitude better than what dedicated NIST-benchmarked fingerprint sensors achieve and roughly six to seven orders of magnitude better than phone-camera fingerphoto EER (§1), for a *lower-information* surface (card texture vs. finger ridges). That gap should be treated with real skepticism absent a disclosed methodology.

**What's technically disclosed, via their patent portfolio (55+ issued patents).** Alitheon's own patents give more insight into the actual mechanism than their marketing pages do. US10,963,670 ("Object change detection and measurement using digital fingerprints") describes: extracting points of interest via a **Laplacian-of-Gaussian-style scale-space blob detector** (explicitly analogous to SIFT/SURF, cited by name in the patent as prior-art-style techniques they build on: "invariant to scale, rotate, translation, illumination, and blur"), representing each as a feature vector, and matching via **thresholded Euclidean/Manhattan distance** between feature vectors of a reference vs. probe capture.[^alitheon-patent] Critically, this patent also describes handling *legitimate change over time* (wear, aging) by distinguishing "class fingerprints" (features shared across many instances of a product class, e.g. currency of the same denomination) from "individual fingerprints" (foreground-only features unique to one object, requiring an initial enrollment/"induction" capture) and quantifying deterioration as the fraction of matched points lost between two captures of the *same* object over time, gated by either a fixed threshold (e.g., 50% point loss) or a learned model.[^alitheon-patent] **This is architecturally the same idea CardVision already implements** — ORB keypoints + BFMatcher + RANSAC in `align.py`, thresholded on inlier count/reprojection error — just with a different (and non-patent-free, pre-2020, hence Alitheon likely used SIFT/SURF-style detectors rather than ORB) choice of local feature detector, and Alitheon layering a learned/threshold-based "how much change is too much" decision on top instead of a hand-tuned SSIM/anomaly-area score. The conceptual approach is not exotic; it's classical CV keypoint matching with product-specific tuning, same family as CardVision's pipeline, not a fundamentally different technology.

**What is NOT disclosed anywhere public**: dataset size, image counts, capture protocol (lighting, distance, angle constraints), what counts as a "trial" in their accuracy denominator, or how they specifically handle glossy/foil trading-card surfaces. Given the patent's emphasis on Laplacian blob detection (interest points concentrated where there's local contrast/texture), a low-texture glossy trading card region would face the same "not enough texture to fingerprint" problem CardVision's `detail_score` heuristic is trying to flag — Alitheon's marketing does not address this limitation for cards specifically.

### 2.2 PSA / Genamint — provenance/resubmission tracking, not point-of-sale buyer verification

PSA acquired Genamint in April 2021.[^psa-genamint] Genamint's disclosed approach (via press coverage, not a public patent — a targeted patent search for "Genamint" turned up nothing published under that name, so its algorithm is proprietary/undisclosed beyond marketing-level description) is to fingerprint each card against **~70 identified anomaly types** — creases, scratches, stains, down to single-compromised-pixel-level print defects — building a per-card signature used to (a) assist human graders in spotting trimming/alteration, and (b) detect **resubmission** of the same physical card (e.g., someone who cracked a slab open hoping for a better grade on regrade).[^psa-genamint][^actionnetwork]

This is a **materially different deployment context** than CardVision's: Genamint runs inside PSA's own controlled grading facility, almost certainly under fixed, calibrated, high-resolution, consistent lighting (a proper capture rig, not a random seller's phone camera in variable home lighting) — the same "provenance/audit trail for a single custodial chain" problem, not "verify two independently-captured phone photos taken by two different untrusted parties days apart," which is the actual CardVision problem. PSA's public-facing verification products (Cert Verification, PSA e-Verification app) are about validating a **cert number against PSA's database**, i.e., "is this a real PSA cert," not "is this physical card the same physical card as an earlier photo" — that image-fingerprint capability is internal to PSA's grading pipeline, not exposed to buyers/sellers as a P2P marketplace tool.[^psa-cert] This confirms there is no existing buyer-facing product doing what CardVision needs to do — the closest comparable production system is Alitheon's, not PSA's.

### 2.3 Entrupy — adjacent problem, partially transferable technique

Entrupy authenticates luxury goods (handbags) as **genuine vs. counterfeit** — a classification problem trained on labeled genuine/counterfeit examples, fundamentally different from CardVision's 1:1 same-object matching (there is no "this specific card is fake" ground truth to train against; a real card photographed twice is the whole problem). Technically: a handheld microscope attachment captures **microscopic material/fabric texture images** (not macro photos), which are classified by a **CNN ensemble, with separate trained models per material/fabric type**, deployed server-side on GPU; low-confidence "unverified" results route to human expert review.[^entrupy-patent][^entrupy-bloomberg] Entrupy reports **99.1% accuracy** in its own marketing, with no independently disclosed FAR/FRR breakdown.[^entrupy-accuracy]

**What transfers to CardVision**: (a) the **microscope-level magnification** requirement — Entrupy's whole premise is that counterfeit-detection-grade texture signal requires optics beyond what a normal phone camera captures at arm's length, which is a data point against expecting phone-camera-only capture to reach dedicated-hardware accuracy levels; (b) the **route-to-human-review on low confidence** pattern, which CardVision's `inconclusive`/`insufficient_detail` rungs already implement structurally, and which §4's calibration plan should lean on explicitly; (c) **per-material-type separate models**, suggesting CardVision may eventually want per-surface-type (foil vs. matte vs. graded-slab-plastic) handling rather than one universal threshold — directly relevant to the foil discussion in §3.4.

What does **not** transfer: Entrupy's core signal (fabric weave/material authenticity at microscope resolution) answers "is this genuine leather/fabric," a question CardVision explicitly is not trying to answer (genuineness is PSA/BGS/CGC's job per the CLAUDE.md scope decision) — it's a nearby but distinct problem shape.

### 2.4 Academic paper-fiber / PUF literature — strong theory, weak transfer to foil/laminated cardstock

- **Buchanan et al., "'Fingerprinting' documents and packaging," Nature 436, 475 (2005).** Foundational result: paper, plastic cards, and product packaging contain a naturally-occurring, effectively unclonable microscopic surface identity, readable with a **low-cost portable laser scanner** (not a phone camera).[^nature2005]
- **Clarkson, Weyrich, Finkelstein, Heninger, Halderman, Felten, "Fingerprinting Blank Paper Using Commodity Scanners," IEEE S&P 2009.** Reconstructs a 3-D surface height model of paper from **four scans at different angles on a commodity flatbed scanner**, robust to handling — explicitly a controlled multi-angle capture protocol, not a single 2-D photo.[^clarkson2009]
- **"Texture to the Rescue: Practical Paper Fingerprinting based on Texture Patterns"** (Seresht et al., arXiv:1705.02510) — this is the one paper in this line of work that specifically targets **camera-based (not scanner-based) paper fingerprinting** intended to be more practical/lower-cost than the laser/flatbed approaches above; it exists precisely because the authors recognized scanner-based capture is impractical for consumer/field use. (Its full technical results were not extractable from the fetched PDF in this pass due to encoding issues; the framing — camera-based practicality vs. Clarkson's scanner requirement — is confirmed by its abstract/title and citation context.) The existence of this paper is itself evidence that the field considers scanner-vs-camera capture a real, nontrivial gap, not an implementation detail.[^texture-rescue]
- **"Identity Document and banknote security forensics: a survey"** (arXiv:1910.08993) situates this whole line of work: it explicitly states that **embedded paper texture is a more reliable fingerprinting source than surface features**, and separately flags that PUF-based banknote authentication needs further study of **resilience to surface tampering** (scratching, folding, crumpling) — i.e., even in the best-studied substrate (paper), robustness to ordinary handling is an open research concern, not solved.[^banknote-survey]

**Transfer assessment to trading cards, stated as reasoning, not as a cited finding (no paper tests trading cardstock specifically):** the theoretical basis (random fiber/print micro-texture is hard to reproduce exactly) is sound and should transfer to the *non-foil, non-laminated* portion of a card's surface. But essentially every result cited above depends on either (a) a dedicated scanning geometry (laser scanner or multi-angle flatbed) that no seller/buyer phone-camera workflow will realistically replicate, or (b) **raw, uncoated paper fiber**, which a glossy or foil-treated trading card partially or fully obscures. This is the single biggest reason to expect CardVision's phone-camera FAR to sit closer to the fingerphoto literature's 6–15% EER range (§1) than to Alitheon's claimed 10⁻⁹ FAR or the paper-PUF literature's lab-controlled results.

## 3. Ranked upgrade recommendations against the existing pipeline

Ordered by expected ROI (accuracy/robustness gain per unit of implementation + compute cost), not by sophistication. For each: **keep / upgrade / add**, the concrete reason, and the specific file/function it touches.

### 3.1 Alignment stage (`align.py`) — **keep ORB+RANSAC as the default; add a fallback, don't replace**

ORB+BFMatcher+Lowe's-ratio+RANSAC is a reasonable choice and, per the patent evidence in §2.1, is architecturally the same family of technique Alitheon itself appears to use (scale/rotation-invariant local keypoints + geometric verification). It is **not** obviously the weak point in the current pipeline for well-lit, roughly-frontal, non-glare captures.

Where it will actually fail, based on the published behavior of learned alternatives:
- **Low light / low contrast**: ORB's FAST-corner detector produces fewer, noisier keypoints as contrast drops; classical detectors in general "suffer from few extracted features and low accuracy due to low brightness, low contrast, and high noise."[^lowlight-superglue] SuperGlue-family learned matchers are reported to achieve "higher pose estimation and registration reliability under challenging lighting or sensor conditions compared to classical matchers."[^lowlight-superglue]
- **Foil glare / specular highlights**: no keypoint detector, classical or learned, is glare-robust on its own — this is a capture-and-preprocessing problem, not a matcher problem (see §3.4).
- **Off-axis capture**: RANSAC-fit homography already handles moderate perspective; extreme off-axis capture is better addressed by tightening the Sell-wizard's guided-capture UX (prompt "hold flat, fill the frame") than by a fancier matcher.

**Recommendation**: keep ORB as the fast default path in `align.py`. Add a **fallback trigger**: when the current alignment gate fails (`inliers < 15` or `reprojection error ≥ 10px`), retry once with a **SIFT** detector (patent-expired since March 7, 2020, now free and included in OpenCV ≥ 4.3 by default[^sift-patent]) before declaring alignment failure. This is a low-cost, low-risk addition (SIFT is already in `opencv-contrib`/mainline OpenCV, no new dependency, same classical-CV mental model as the rest of the pipeline) that should recover some of the poor-lighting cases without taking on a deep-learning inference dependency. **Do not** reach for SuperGlue/LoFTR/DISK/D2-Net yet — HPatches benchmark numbers show real gains over classical descriptors specifically on illumination/viewpoint-heavy pairs (e.g. DISK reaching 52.3% vs. D2-Net's 23.2% homography-estimation accuracy at a 3px threshold on HPatches[^disk-hpatches]), but they require a GPU-inference dependency, added latency, and a model-serving story that doesn't exist anywhere else in this Go/Python-adjacent stack yet — that's real added operational complexity for a stage that isn't demonstrably the current bottleneck. Revisit only after §4's calibration work identifies alignment failures (not surface-comparison failures) as the dominant error mode.

### 3.2 Residual/surface stage (`residual.py`) — **keep SSIM as the cheap baseline; the real gap is anomaly *characterization*, not detection**

SSIM+CLAHE+thresholded-diff is a legitimate "cheap CV baseline," and the pipeline's own handling of registration-ringing (Gaussian blur to suppress thin alignment-halo artifacts while preserving wider, spatially coherent genuine marks) shows real engineering care already went into this stage. The bigger issue is conceptual, not algorithmic: **SSIM was designed to measure perceived image-quality degradation, not to distinguish "this is the same object with new wear" from "this is a different object."** Both produce a diff map; the current pipeline's only lever for telling them apart is anomaly *area fraction* against a single global, uncalibrated ceiling constant.

This is exactly the shape of problem industrial visual inspection solves with **one-class / cold-start anomaly detection**, which is directly relevant because — like CardVision — it's a domain with abundant "normal" examples and effectively no usable "defect" examples to train a classifier against:
- **PatchCore** (Roth et al., 2021): builds a coreset memory bank of normal-patch deep-feature embeddings (from a pretrained backbone, e.g. ImageNet ResNet), then scores test patches by distance to their nearest memory-bank neighbor; reports up to **99.1% image-level AUROC** and pixel-level AUROC of 98.1% on MVTec AD, the standard industrial-defect benchmark.[^patchcore]
- **PaDiM** (Defard et al., 2020): models per-patch-position feature distributions (multivariate Gaussian) from a pretrained CNN, distance-scores test patches against that distribution; a predecessor to PatchCore with somewhat lower reported accuracy (pixelwise AUROC 97.5 vs. PatchCore's 98.1).[^patchcore]

**Recommendation**: this is the highest-ROI upgrade in the whole pipeline, but frame it correctly — it's not "replace SSIM," it's "add a second, complementary anomaly *scoring* signal that CardVision's `residual.py`/`score.py` can fuse with the SSIM diff instead of relying on SSIM's diff-map thresholding alone." Concretely: after alignment (still ORB, §3.1), extract patch-level embeddings from a small pretrained CNN (a ResNet-18/-34 or MobileNet backbone is enough — these methods deliberately don't require fine-tuning) over the *reference* card image only, build a small memory bank, and score the *probe* image's patches against it the same way PatchCore does. This directly attacks the pipeline's current biggest known weakness — the uncalibrated `_ANOMALY_FRACTION_CEILING` in `score.py` (§3.3) — because a learned-embedding anomaly score is a *continuous, better-separated* signal than raw SSIM diff-area, which should make the eventual calibration in §4 land on a materially better ceiling/threshold than tuning SSIM's area-fraction alone could achieve. This is meaningfully more implementation cost than the alignment fallback (a pretrained-model inference dependency, one new package), but it's the stage where CardVision's problem shape most directly matches an already-solved, well-benchmarked problem (MVTec-AD-style industrial inspection), so the expected accuracy gain per engineering-hour is the highest of any recommendation in this report.

**Do not** reach for full autoencoder/reconstruction-based anomaly detection or one-class SVM-on-deep-features as a *first* move — PatchCore specifically was built to beat both of those older approaches on the same benchmark family, so starting with PatchCore-style patch-memory scoring skips a generation of now-superseded techniques.[^patchcore]

### 3.3 Scoring stage (`score.py`) — **keep the 6-way decision ladder structure; the constants, not the architecture, are the problem**

The `match`/`likely_match`/`inconclusive`/`likely_mismatch`/`mismatch`/`insufficient_detail` ladder, the quality-tier score cap, and the "weaker face wins" front/back combination rule are all sound *design* decisions — this is the right shape for a system that has to communicate calibrated uncertainty to a support agent or a dispute reviewer, matching this codebase's own stated principle elsewhere (CLAUDE.md §5.2, "one state machine, not scattered booleans," applied here to a decision ladder instead of an order state machine). **Nothing here needs architectural change.** What needs to change is exactly what the code's own comments already flag: `_ANOMALY_FRACTION_CEILING` and `_DETAIL_FLOOR` are placeholders. That's a data problem, addressed in §4, not a code problem.

One structural addition worth making once §3.2's embedding-based anomaly signal exists: fuse it into `match_score` as a fourth weighted term (or replace `residual.ssim_score`'s weight with a blend of SSIM + patch-anomaly-score) rather than bolting it on as a separate gate — keeps the "one score, one ladder" property instead of adding a second, parallel decision path that a future maintainer has to reason about independently.

### 3.4 Foil/holo handling — currently unhandled; **add capture-side mitigation before algorithm-side mitigation**

The pipeline has no foil-specific logic today. The industrial-inspection literature on reflective-surface imaging is consistent on the fix being **capture-side, not post-hoc software**: polarization filtering (cross-polarized light source + camera filter) is the standard technique for suppressing specular highlights on reflective/metallic surfaces before the image is ever processed, not after.[^polarization-review][^polarization-metal] Multiple recent papers (2023–2025) on polarization-based specular-highlight removal for reflective/metallic industrial parts confirm this is an active, still-evolving research area even in controlled factory settings — i.e., even *with* dedicated equipment this isn't fully solved, so expecting a pure-software fix against uncontrolled seller-phone glare is optimistic.[^polarization-metal2]

**Recommendation, ranked**:
1. **Cheapest, do first**: capture-flow guidance, not new CV — prompt the seller (Sell wizard, Step 2 Photos) to angle the card and/or take the photo with an off-axis light source (avoid direct on-axis flash into a holo surface), and/or capture **two frames at different tilt angles** for foil cards specifically, keeping whichever frame has the smaller glare/blown-highlight area (measurable cheaply as % of pixels near 255 in a luminance channel) as the reference. This requires no new inference stack, just a UX change plus a simple pixel-saturation heuristic already within reach of the existing OpenCV toolkit.
2. **Medium cost**: a **glare mask** — detect blown-highlight regions (simple luminance threshold + morphological close, similar machinery to the existing anomaly-region-finding in `residual.py`) and **exclude them from both alignment keypoint scoring and SSIM/anomaly comparison** rather than trying to "see through" them. This turns foil glare from a source of false anomalies into a simply-ignored region, at the cost of reduced sensitivity in that region — an acceptable tradeoff given the goal is avoiding false accepts/rejects, not maximizing coverage.
3. **Higher cost, defer**: true polarization capture (a physical polarizing filter on the capture device) is the technique with the most direct research support[^polarization-review], but requires seller-side hardware (a cheap clip-on polarizer) that can't be assumed for a P2P marketplace's photo-upload flow — reasonable to flag as a "pro seller" accessory recommendation later, not a v1 requirement.

### 3.5 Multi-frame capture / anti-spoof — **not currently present; add a lightweight version, don't port full face-liveness machinery**

Today's single front+back photo pair has no defense against someone matching a *photo of a photo* (or a screen) instead of the real object, and no defense against submitting someone else's earlier reference photo as if it were a fresh capture. The face-recognition and document-forensics liveness literature offers two techniques that are both well-suited to a low-friction mobile capture flow and both already partially learnable from CardVision's own data flow:

- **Passive artifact detection**: replay/recapture attacks (photographing a screen or printed photo instead of the real object) leave detectable **moiré patterns** (screen-refresh/pixel-grid interference) and characteristic **specular reflection differences** from a flat digital display vs. a real 3-D object's surface.[^document-liveness][^moire] This is a classifier that can run passively on the *existing* capture with no added seller friction — no explicit citation exists yet for its accuracy specifically on trading cards, but the mechanism (screen pixel grids create predictable frequency-domain artifacts) is well-established in the document/ID-verification literature.[^id-pad-review]
- **Active challenge-response, kept minimal**: face-liveness systems use randomized prompts (turn head, blink) specifically so a static photo/video-replay can't be pre-recorded to pass;[^liveness-challenge] the trading-card analogue that respects this codebase's own stated constraint ("not going to tolerate a 30-second face-ID-style ritual for a $5 card") is a **single extra frame**, not a video: prompt the seller for one additional photo at a different tilt/angle (which the Sell wizard should already be prompting for foil handling per §3.4 — this can piggyback on the same capture step rather than being a separate ritual) and verify the **specular highlight position moved consistent with a rotated 3-D object** rather than staying fixed (which would indicate a flat photo of a photo being re-angled in front of the camera, since a truly flat surface's highlight geometry does not change the same way a card's does under tilt). This is a genuinely cheap add — it reuses the same glare/highlight-region detection built for §3.4 — and provides real, if modest, protection against the single most likely spoof (submitting a stock/stolen photo as a "live" capture) without imposing a video-capture UX.

**Do not** build full depth-sensing or 3D-mask-style liveness infrastructure (irrelevant threat model — nobody is 3D-printing a fake trading card to spoof a camera) or adopt the face-anti-spoofing field's heavier CNN-based passive detectors wholesale; the moiré/specular-consistency checks above are the right-sized subset for this threat model.

## 4. Calibration plan

The codebase's own comments are honest that `_ANOMALY_FRACTION_CEILING` and `_DETAIL_FLOOR` in `score.py` are "placeholder values needing calibration against real photos of pristine vs. worn cards" — not yet validated against a labeled dataset. A small team can establish real FAR/FRR numbers for these constants without a large pre-existing labeled corpus, using the same bootstrapping pattern the active-learning literature describes as standard practice for exactly this "expensive labels, cheap unlabeled data" situation.[^active-learning]

**Step 1 — Generate genuine hard negatives cheaply, in-house.** The single most valuable dataset CardVision needs is pairs of **different physical copies of the same bulk-common card** (same set, same card number, same print run — i.e., what a swapped-card fraud would actually look like), since that's the exact confusion CardVision must resolve and no public dataset provides it. This is inexpensive to source: buy a set of ~50–100 commons/bulk cards from the same set (a few dollars each on the secondary market), photograph each one twice (simulating "listing photo" and "delivery photo," varying lighting/angle naturally between the two sessions the way a real seller-then-buyer photo pair would), and treat every cross-card comparison within the same SKU as a labeled hard negative and every same-card, two-capture comparison as a labeled positive. This single small physical purchase (a few hundred dollars, one afternoon of photography) produces a dataset that directly answers "what does `anomaly_area_fraction` look like for two genuinely different physical objects of the identical print" — precisely the number `_ANOMALY_FRACTION_CEILING` needs.

**Step 2 — Generate genuine positives across realistic wear/handling.** Take the same physical cards from Step 1 and re-photograph them after simulated realistic handling (light shuffling, a trip through a top-loader and back, a few days' delay between shots to get natural lighting variance) to build the positive (same-object) distribution under real-world capture variance, not just back-to-back photos taken in the same session/lighting — the latter would understate real FRR because production listing-vs-delivery photos are never taken under matched conditions.

**Step 3 — Compute an actual FAR/FRR/EER curve.** With Steps 1–2's labeled pairs, sweep `match_score`'s threshold and plot FAR vs. FRR the standard biometric way,[^signzy] rather than picking a single constant by inspection. This is also the point at which `_DETAIL_FLOOR` gets calibrated: bucket the Step 1/2 pairs by their `detail_score`, and confirm the `insufficient_detail` rung actually triggers on the low-texture cases (glossy/foil cards) where the genuine-pair and hard-negative-pair score distributions overlap the most — that overlap point is the empirical definition of "not enough detail to trust a match-leaning result," replacing the current guessed floor.

**Step 4 — Route real inconclusive cases to a human-review feedback loop.** Once live, every `inconclusive` decision (and, initially, a random audit sample of `match`/`mismatch` decisions too, to catch calibration drift) should be queued for a human reviewer (support/trust-and-safety staff, not necessarily an expert grader — the task is "do these two photos look like the same physical card," which a briefly-trained non-specialist can do) to label. This is the active-learning pattern of using model uncertainty to prioritize which examples are worth the cost of a human label, which the literature reports can cut labeling effort dramatically relative to labeling everything or nothing.[^active-learning] Feed those human labels back into periodic re-runs of Step 3's threshold sweep — this turns calibration from a one-time exercise into an ongoing, ever-more-accurate feedback loop, and is the only realistic path to eventually justifying real FAR/FRR numbers in user-facing copy per §1.

**Step 5 — Only after Steps 1–4 produce a real labeled set (order of hundreds to low thousands of pairs), consider the metric-learning path** (Siamese/triplet-loss embedding network, trained explicitly on the same hard-negative shape as Step 1 — same-SKU, different physical copy — the way FaceNet's own semi-hard-negative-mining strategy was central to making its embeddings actually separate look-alike faces[^facenet]). This is explicitly a **later** step, not a v1 recommendation: triplet/contrastive networks are known to need enough hard negatives to make mining meaningful, and CardVision doesn't have that yet. Architecturally, note this is still fundamentally a **1:1 verification problem** even with learned embeddings — pgvector-style approximate nearest-neighbor search is built for 1:N retrieval-at-scale and would be solving a problem CardVision doesn't have;[^pgvector] a straight cosine/Euclidean distance between exactly two embeddings, computed inline, is both simpler and the architecturally correct fit here. Don't add a vector index for this.

## 6. Can AuctionHous build this in-house with just a phone? ("Gaussian Technology," LiDAR, and the real resolution gap)

**Translating the question.** "Gaussian Technology" is not a term of art. The most plausible real referent, given the surrounding phrasing ("new technology of computer vision through iPhone scanning"), is **3D Gaussian Splatting** (Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler, Drettakis, SIGGRAPH 2023) — the technique behind the current wave of consumer "3D scanning" apps (Polycam, Luma AI, Scaniverse) that made phone-based 3D capture a mainstream buzzword over the last two years.[^gsplat-paper] It is unlikely the user means "Gaussian blur" or "Gaussian process regression" (neither is marketed as an "iPhone scanning" technology); this section proceeds on the Gaussian Splatting reading but also covers every other plausible real technique that matches the *actual goal stated* — a phone-only way to recover a height-map-like surface fingerprint — regardless of what it's called.

### 6.1 3D Gaussian Splatting — wrong tool, by three to five orders of magnitude

**What it actually does.** 3DGS represents a scene as a cloud of thousands to millions of translucent 3D Gaussian "blobs" (each with position, covariance/shape, color, and opacity), optimized by differentiable rendering against a set of ordinary 2D photos taken from many viewpoints around the subject, then rendered in real time via splatting rather than ray-marching.[^gsplat-paper] It was built to solve **novel-view-synthesis** (render a photorealistic new camera angle of a scene you didn't literally photograph) at real-time speed — a rendering-quality problem, not a metrology problem. The original paper's own follow-up work makes this explicit: because 3D Gaussians are volumetric primitives, they "conflict with the thin nature of surfaces" and 3DGS "does not natively model surface normals, essential for high-quality surface reconstruction" — later papers (e.g. 2D Gaussian Splatting) exist specifically to patch this geometric-accuracy gap, which confirms the base method was never geometry-accuracy-first.[^gsplat-2d]

**Measured accuracy, in real units.** This is the number that answers the question. A comparative study benchmarking Gaussian-Splatting-derived geometry against LiDAR ground truth reports a **mean geometric error of 7.82 cm ± 11.49 cm**; even after constraining the reconstruction with surveyed ground-control points, accuracy only improves to roughly **3–5 cm**, and the surveying/engineering literature is explicit that "Gaussian Splatting currently lacks the required centimeter- or millimeter-level accuracy for reliable, measurement-grade output."[^gsplat-accuracy] The single most favorable number found — **1–2 mm** error — only appears when the pipeline is *initialized from* a dedicated terrestrial LiDAR scanner (a Leica RTC360, industrial survey-grade hardware costing tens of thousands of dollars) as reference geometry, not from phone photos alone.[^gsplat-accuracy] Even at that best case, 1–2 mm is still **three to four orders of magnitude coarser** than the tens-of-microns feature size this report's §1 identifies as "fingerprint-grade" card texture.

**A structural mismatch, not just a resolution one (reasoning, not a cited finding).** Gaussian Splatting's whole value proposition is triangulating geometry *and* appearance from parallax across many viewpoints of a genuinely 3D object. A trading card is, for this purpose, essentially a flat 2D surface — there is no meaningful multi-view parallax to triangulate on a print pattern sitting on a ~0.5 mm-thick card. Photographing a card from 40 angles the way you'd photograph a statue would mostly just give 3DGS forty near-identical, nearly-parallel views of the same flat plane — not the kind of geometric diversity the method needs to do anything useful. This is inference from how the method works, not a published test on cards specifically (none was found), but it follows directly from the mechanism: **Gaussian Splatting is very likely solving a problem a flat card doesn't have (macro-scale 3D shape) while being far too coarse for the problem it does have (micron-scale surface relief).** Consumer app documentation confirms the coarse end of this independently: reviewers note that even the highest-quality mobile Gaussian-Splatting captures target objects in the **30–100 cm size range**, and that phone-based LiDAR-assisted capture (the highest-fidelity mobile input available) is "disappointing for detailed scans... great for surveying... not for detailed, photorealistic meshes."[^gsplat-mobile] **Verdict: rule this out entirely for CardVision's stated goal.**

### 6.2 Photometric stereo / shape-from-shading — the real match for "height map from a phone," with real caveats

This is the technique the user is actually picturing, described precisely: recover a **surface normal map** (and, by integrating it, a height/relief map) of a static object from **multiple ordinary 2D photos taken from a fixed camera position, varying only the lighting direction between shots** — no special sensor, just a camera and a way to move the light (a phone's flash fired from a few different physical positions, an LED ring with sequentially-fired lights, or a lamp moved by hand between shots).[^photostereo-overview] It is a decades-old, well-established computer vision technique (not new, unlike Gaussian Splatting), which is itself a point in its favor for a small team to implement without deep-learning infrastructure.

**What's needed for a phone-only version, and what's been built already:**
- "Shape-from-Shifting" is an academic, phone-native implementation built specifically for this constraint: a "simple, low-cost and streamlined photometric stereo framework for scanning planar surfaces with a consumer mobile device," using nothing but the phone's own camera and flash across a sequence of shots.[^photostereo-mobile] The word "planar surfaces" is notable — this is explicitly the geometry class a trading card belongs to, unlike the multi-view 3D-object case Gaussian Splatting targets.
- A separate mobile photometric-stereo system using **top-down LED illumination removes the need for tight synchronization between light and camera, tolerates ambient light, and works on moving subjects** — practically relevant because it relaxes the "must be in a dark room with a rig" constraint — but reports **depth errors around 2.7 mm** on centimeter-scale objects.[^photostereo-led] That 2.7 mm figure is for a *hand/face-scale* object measuring gross 3D shape (not flat print-defect relief), so it is not a direct stand-in for card-texture precision, but it's a useful anchor: even a purpose-built academic mobile photometric-stereo rig, under favorable conditions, is reporting millimeter-scale depth error, not micron-scale.
- **The resolution ceiling is not the lighting math, it's the same lateral camera resolution problem as everywhere else in this report.** Photometric stereo estimates a normal *per pixel* — it cannot see detail finer than what the underlying camera can spatially resolve in the first place. This is reasoning, not a distinct citation: the technique adds *relief/shading sensitivity* (can detect a shallow scratch a flat-lit photo would miss), but it does not increase lateral resolution beyond the camera+lens system's own limit (§6.6's table). **This means photometric stereo is a genuine, real upgrade over the current single-flat-lighting-condition capture CardVision uses today, but it only pays off when paired with better lateral optics (§6.5), not as a standalone fix.**
- **Known glossy/foil failure mode (directly relevant to this report's §3.4 foil discussion).** Photometric stereo's underlying image-formation model in almost all practical implementations assumes **Lambertian (diffuse) reflectance** — intensity depends only on the angle between surface normal and light, with no specular component.[^photostereo-overview] A glossy or foil card surface violates this assumption directly: specular highlights move with the light source in a way the diffuse model doesn't predict, producing bad or NaN normal estimates exactly in the specular regions — the same foil/glare problem flagged in §3.4 of this report, now appearing as a *harder* failure mode for photometric stereo specifically than for the existing SSIM pipeline, because photometric stereo's whole signal comes from trusting shading changes that specular reflection actively corrupts. Uncalibrated/mobile-display photometric-stereo variants exist that try to work outside a dark room,[^photostereo-diff] but none of the sources found claim robustness on foil/holographic card stock specifically — treat this as an open risk, not a solved problem.

**Verdict: real, viable, and the closest match to what the user described — but it is a second capture modality on top of the existing pipeline (multiple lighting-direction shots, ideally with a fixed phone position), not a drop-in replacement for a single photo, and its foil-surface failure mode needs its own testing before trusting it on holo cards.**

### 6.3 Reflectance Transformation Imaging (RTI) — the closest production analogue, at hobbyist cost, proven on coins

RTI (invented at HP Labs in 2000, standard practice in museum/cultural-heritage documentation since) is, in effect, **photometric stereo's field-deployed cousin**: capture dozens of ordinary photos of a static small object from a fixed camera position while a light source is moved to many known angular positions around it (a hemispherical dome of fixed LEDs is common, but so is a single hand-held flash with metal-ball position tracking), then combine them into an interactive "relightable" image plus a per-pixel surface-normal map.[^rti-chsopensource] It is explicitly used today to document **coins, seals, and manuscripts** — physically the closest existing category to a trading card in both scale and value-per-object — and museum documentation states plainly that RTI results "can be used as fingerprints to identify unique objects and recognize surface alterations," which is close to a word-for-word match to CardVision's own stated goal.[^rti-chsopensource]

**Cost and complexity, concretely.** A fully automated DIY RTI dome rig (Arduino-controlled LED sequencing, so capture is a single button press rather than manually repositioning a light dozens of times) can be built for **under about $600, plus a camera** — well within reach for a startup, not industrial-scale spending.[^rti-arduino] Multiple published DIY builds exist at this budget level, including a well-documented Hackaday project.[^rti-hackaday] Software to process the captured photo sets into relightable/normal-map output (e.g., the free tools from Cultural Heritage Imaging, the organization that helped standardize RTI) is mature and free.[^rti-chi]

**Does it reach microscopic scale, and has it been used on card-like objects?** Yes to the first — a documented "microscopic-level" RTI variant exists specifically for **artifact use-wear analysis** (i.e., reading fine tool-mark and surface-wear detail well below what a standard dome RTI resolves), extending the RTI concept down toward the resolution regime CardVision actually needs.[^rti-microscopic] On the second question, the honest answer is **no direct trading-card (or comparable modern printed-cardstock collectible) RTI study was found** in this research pass — RTI's proven collectible use case is ancient/worn coins and manuscripts, which are physically similar in scale but have very different surface properties (metal relief and ink-on-parchment vs. printed cardstock, foil lamination, and glossy coating). Extending RTI to trading cards specifically is a reasonable, low-cost experiment to run in-house, not a proven, off-the-shelf result to assume will just work.

**Verdict: this is the most credible "build it yourself, don't license Alitheon" path in this entire report — proven methodology, hobbyist budget, closest real-world precedent to CardVision's actual goal — but it is genuinely new engineering work (a lighting rig, a capture protocol, and validation specifically on foil/glossy modern cardstock, none of which exists yet), not a feature flag on the current pipeline.**

### 6.4 iPhone LiDAR / TrueDepth — a red herring for this specific use case, with real numbers to show it

**LiDAR.** The iPhone's LiDAR scanner outputs a depth map of roughly **256×192 points per frame (~49,000 points)**, and independent accuracy studies report on the order of **±1–3 cm horizontal / several-mm-to-1cm vertical accuracy** in general use, with one close-range study measuring a **~5 mm mean deviation** and construction-focused testing noting geometry finer than **about 1 cm falls below the sensor's effective resolution** entirely.[^lidar-accuracy] That is roughly **three to four orders of magnitude too coarse** to detect a scratch or print defect at the tens-of-microns scale this report's §1 identifies as fingerprint-grade. LiDAR on iPhone is built for room-scale AR geometry and furniture-scale object scanning, not micro-texture — it is simply the wrong instrument, not a matter of clever processing recovering more from it.

**TrueDepth.** The front-camera structured-light sensor (used for Face ID) is somewhat better documented and slightly more capable at very close range, but still nowhere near the target: independent measurement studies report **RMS accuracy around 0.86 mm ± 0.31 mm** and **flatness/straightness errors around 0.4 mm** in careful lab conditions;[^truedepth-accuracy] one source claims a best-case minimum resolvable feature of **~0.1 mm** at its designed 150–170 mm working distance.[^truedepth-resolution] Even taking the single most favorable number (0.1 mm = 100 microns) at face value, that is still roughly **one to two orders of magnitude coarser** than tens-of-microns-scale card texture, and TrueDepth's structured-light dot pattern and working distance are both tuned for face geometry, not held-close flat objects. **Verdict: neither sensor is remotely close to viable for this use case — treat "iPhone scanning" claims involving LiDAR or TrueDepth as a dead end for surface-texture fingerprinting specifically, though LiDAR/TrueDepth-based rough shape/thickness checks could have a minor, unrelated fraud-detection use (e.g., confirming a "graded slab" listing photo shows an object of plausible slab thickness) — a different, much coarser problem than this section is answering.**

### 6.5 Clip-on macro/microscope lenses — the practical hardware path, and the missing piece in every other option above

Cheap ($10–50) clip-on macro and "microscope" lens attachments for phones are widely sold and, unlike every sensor discussed in §6.4, some are independently documented as reaching into the actual target resolution range. The **Blips** line (SmartMicroOptics, a well-reviewed, Kickstarter-funded, multiply-covered product — not a no-name marketplace listing) specifies **Blips Micro at ~4.5-micron resolvable detail and Blips Ultra at ~3.5 microns**, at a roughly 6 mm working distance.[^macro-blips] Broader clip-on macro lens listings claim 10–40x magnification for general close-up work, with dedicated multi-lens "microscope kits" reaching 100–200x for a similarly low price.[^macro-general] For comparison and a sanity check on plausibility: a **purpose-built, ~$1,000+ professional macro lens for a full DSLR** (Canon's MP-E 65mm, dedicated 1–5× macro glass at f/2.8) has a diffraction-limited theoretical resolution around **0.7 microns**, but *measured, real-world* resolution in practice is about **4 microns** — optical aberrations and practical shooting conditions eat almost an order of magnitude of the theoretical limit even on genuinely professional glass.[^macro-diffraction] That both anchors expectations (a cheap clip-on lens claiming ~3.5–4.5 microns is, remarkably, in the same ballpark as measured performance from dedicated pro macro optics) and is a reason for healthy skepticism of the clip-on vendor number until independently tested — vendor-claimed resolution figures for $20 lenses should be verified in-house before being trusted as a design input.

**Why this matters more than any sensor in §6.4**: none of iPhone's built-in cameras, with or without a clip-on lens, produce a literal height map on their own — a lens only fixes *lateral* resolution (§6.6). The genuinely new capability a macro lens unlocks is getting the base camera's pixel grid down into the feature-size range that actually matters, which is the prerequisite for either (a) a sharper 2D image for the existing SSIM/anomaly pipeline in §3, or (b) usable input to photometric stereo (§6.2) or RTI (§6.3), neither of which can produce a meaningful height map faster than their weakest link — and today, that weakest link is lateral resolution, not the lighting math.

**Verdict: yes, this is real, cheap, and plausible — but it's an *enabling accessory* for §6.2/§6.3, not a standalone fingerprinting technology by itself.** A macro lens with a single flat-lit photo just gives CardVision a sharper ordinary photo (useful, feeds directly into the existing pipeline's alignment/residual stages per §3), not a height map — the height-map capability specifically requires pairing it with multiple lighting directions (photometric stereo/RTI).

| **DIY photometric stereo (phone + moved flash/LED, no macro lens)** | Depth error **~2.7 mm** reported for a mobile academic rig on cm-scale objects (hand/face scale, not micro-texture) — bounded above by whatever lateral camera resolution feeds it | [^photostereo-led] |
| **DIY RTI rig ($600, cultural-heritage grade)** | Bounded by the camera/lens used to capture it (same numbers as the relevant row above); RTI's own contribution is relief/normal sensitivity, not raw lateral resolution — proven down to microscopic/use-wear scale in a documented specialized variant | [^rti-microscopic] |

**Reading the table**: the two consumer-hardware options that land anywhere near the tens-of-microns target are the **clip-on macro lens** (a real, cheap, independently-documented number) and, by extension, **any capture pipeline built on top of one** (RTI-style or photometric-stereo-style multi-light capture using that lens). Every phone-native sensor — the plain camera at native focus, LiDAR, TrueDepth — and Gaussian Splatting are between two and five orders of magnitude too coarse, with Gaussian Splatting the single worst fit of anything researched (wrong scale *and* wrong data modality, per §6.1's parallax argument).

### 6.7 Verdict: the realistic in-house build path, ranked

1. **Do first, lowest cost, highest near-term ROI: keep refining the existing phone-photo pipeline from §3 of this report.** Nothing in this section changes that conclusion — PatchCore-style anomaly scoring, the SIFT alignment fallback, and glare-region masking are all real, buildable-now improvements that don't require new hardware, and §1's honest FAR/FRR framing still applies regardless of capture method. This remains the correct v1 path.
2. **The one genuinely promising "build our own microscopic fingerprint" R&D bet, if AuctionHous wants to pursue it: a $20–50 clip-on macro lens (§6.5) combined with a simple DIY multi-light capture protocol (§6.2's phone-flash-position or §6.3's RTI-style approach), validated specifically on foil and non-foil cardstock before committing.** This is the only path in this entire section whose component resolution numbers are actually in the right ballpark for "fingerprint-grade" card texture, it's proven methodology (RTI, decades of cultural-heritage use, on physically comparable objects — coins), and it's cheap enough ($600 or less) to prototype without a funding decision. It is **real new engineering work** — a capture rig or a very deliberate multi-shot phone protocol, new alignment/normal-map processing code, and original validation on glossy/foil trading cardstock that no one has published — not a quick win, and its foil-surface failure mode (§6.2) is an open risk that needs testing before trusting it on holo cards specifically, which are a meaningful fraction of valuable modern trading card listings.
3. **Skip entirely: iPhone LiDAR and TrueDepth (§6.4).** Two to five orders of magnitude too coarse; no amount of software cleverness recovers information the sensor never captured.
4. **Skip entirely: 3D Gaussian Splatting / "Gaussian Technology" (§6.1).** Wrong scale (centimeters, not microns) and arguably the wrong data modality for a flat object with no exploitable multi-view parallax. This is very likely what the user encountered in consumer 3D-scanning-app marketing, and it is the least suited technique of everything researched in this report for CardVision's actual goal.

Bottom line for the user's direct question: **a plain iPhone camera or its built-in "3D scanning" features (LiDAR, TrueDepth, or Gaussian-Splatting-based apps) cannot recover a genuine microscopic height-map fingerprint of a card — that capability requires adding cheap external optics (a macro lens) and a multi-light capture protocol (photometric stereo/RTI), which is real, low-budget, buildable R&D, not something the iPhone already does out of the box.** Building that in-house is plausible at hobbyist cost and is the one path researched here that could genuinely reduce dependence on licensing Alitheon-style technology — but it is a v2 research bet to prototype and validate on real foil and non-foil cards, not a v1 replacement for the phone-photo pipeline already recommended in §3.

### 6.8 Locked capture and computational photography — the one lever not yet examined

**Framing the question.** §6.6's iPhone-native resolution estimate (5–30 microns/pixel at macro focus) was an *optical* ceiling — what the lens and sensor geometry can physically deliver. This subsection asks a different question: independent of that optical ceiling, does the iPhone's default **software** pipeline (Smart HDR, Deep Fusion) throw away real high-frequency detail the sensor did capture, and does locking settings / shooting ProRAW recover it? Short answer: **yes, this is a real, previously-missed, and genuinely fixable software problem, but it does not change the optical ceiling established in §6.6 — it only affects whether CardVision gets to keep the detail that ceiling allows.**

**What "locking" actually does (real, narrow capability).** iOS's AVFoundation framework exposes real manual control: an app can lock focus, exposure, and white balance instead of letting continuous autofocus/auto-exposure re-hunt between the reference and probe shots (`AVCaptureDevice`'s locked-focus and `setWhiteBalanceModeLockedWithDeviceWhiteBalanceGains` APIs, standard since iOS 8).[^avfoundation-manual] This is straightforward to build into the Sell-wizard/receiving-photo capture flow and is unambiguously good practice for CardVision regardless of everything else in this section: it removes a source of *inconsistency between the two captures being compared* (different auto-exposure or focus-hunt decisions on listing day vs. delivery day), which is a real, if modest, contributor to false rejects — but it is a **consistency fix, not a resolution unlock**. Locking focus/exposure does not make the lens resolve finer detail than it otherwise would.

**The actual finding: Apple's computational pipeline is a multi-frame *fusion* process, and that process is where real detail can get thrown away, separate from the lens.** Apple's own WWDC documentation describes ProRAW as capturing the same multi-frame Smart HDR/Deep Fusion/Night Mode fusion pipeline's output, but preserving it in an editable raw-like format rather than baking it into a finished HEIC.[^proraw-wwdc] The reporting on Deep Fusion's actual mechanism (via Apple's own briefings to press at launch, not independent teardown, but a substantially more detailed technical account than typical marketing copy) describes: the camera captures **four short-exposure frames, four "secondary" standard frames, and one longer exposure** *before* the shutter is even pressed, merges the standard and long frames into a "synthetic long" exposure, then runs the **Neural Engine pixel-by-pixel across all ~24 million pixels**, per Apple's own description, applying **different processing to different semantic regions of the frame** (e.g., skin, hair, sky get different treatment) via multiple neural networks, picking the "best" pixels from across the frame stack for detail and noise.[^deepfusion-mechanism] This is precisely the mechanism that should concern CardVision: a per-region, denoising-and-detail-optimizing neural fusion process, tuned and likely trained on typical photographic subjects (faces, skies, fabric), is exactly the kind of system that can misclassify **fine, low-contrast, semi-random print-defect/micro-scratch texture as noise to be smoothed away** rather than signal to be preserved — because from the standpoint of a noise-reduction network trained on ordinary photos, a faint scratch and sensor noise can look statistically similar (small, high-frequency, low-amplitude, spatially incoherent-ish deviations). **This specific claim — that Deep Fusion misclassifies card micro-texture as noise — is this report's own inference, not a directly cited finding**; no source found tested Deep Fusion's output against a card-texture ground truth specifically. But it follows directly from the documented mechanism (opaque, semantic-region-aware, multi-frame denoising optimized for conventional photo subjects) applied to a subject (faint incidental print-texture) that pipeline was never designed to preserve, and it is consistent with the general, well-known photography-community complaint that Apple's computational pipeline over-smooths fine textures (skin pores, fabric weave, foliage) in ordinary use — a complaint documented in hands-on comparisons even though a rigorous card-texture-specific study doesn't exist.[^deepfusion-mechanism]

**Does ProRAW + locked capture recover it?** Partially, and this is the actionable part. ProRAW's stated purpose is explicitly to give the photographer **"the depth and flexibility of a raw format"** while still benefiting from multi-frame capture — Apple's own materials describe ProRAW files as carrying computational metadata (e.g., an `AppleNoiseReductionStrength` tag) rather than an irreversibly baked-in final image, meaning some of Deep Fusion/Smart HDR's decisions are stored as **adjustable parameters rather than destructively applied pixels**.[^proraw-wwdc] This means capturing in ProRAW and then processing CardVision's own images through a **custom, minimal-smoothing raw pipeline** (rather than accepting Apple's own default HEIC rendering, which is optimized for pleasing ordinary photos, not for preserving faint anomalies) is a real, buildable lever: it should recover some of the fine detail an out-of-the-box HEIC/JPEG capture would have smoothed away. It does **not** change the underlying optical/diffraction resolution ceiling from §6.6 — ProRAW is still built from the same lens and sensor, at the same pixel pitch, at the same working distance. **Recommendation: capture CardVision reference/probe photos in ProRAW (available on iPhone 12 Pro and later) with focus/exposure/white-balance locked via AVFoundation, and process the raw data through CardVision's own minimal-processing pipeline rather than accepting Apple's default HEIC output.** This is a legitimate, low-cost addition to the Sell-wizard capture flow (§3 of this report) — a software/capture-protocol change, not new hardware — and should be treated as complementary to, not a substitute for, the macro-lens/photometric-stereo path in §6.5–6.7, since it addresses a different failure mode (software over-smoothing) than the one macro lenses address (raw optical resolution).

**Blackmagic — the user's causal story is wrong, and worth correcting directly.** Blackmagic Design's cinema cameras (Pocket Cinema Camera, URSA) are well-regarded for image quality, but **"locking resolution and zoom" is not the mechanism responsible, and manual/locked controls are standard cinematography workflow, not a resolution trick.** What actually makes these cameras capable:
- **Sensor size, an order of magnitude larger than an iPhone's.** The Pocket Cinema Camera 4K uses an 18.96mm × 10mm Four Thirds sensor; the 6K models use an even larger Super 35 sensor.[^blackmagic-specs] An iPhone's main sensor is roughly a 1/1.28-inch type (~9.8mm × 7.3mm for the 15 Pro's 48MP sensor)[^iphone-sensor] — meaning even Blackmagic's *smaller* Four Thirds option has roughly **2–3× the sensor area** of an iPhone's main sensor, and Super 35 is larger still. Larger sensor area at a comparable megapixel count means larger individual photosites, which primarily helps low-light/dynamic-range performance — it is not, by itself, what determines microns-per-pixel-at-the-subject for a macro shot (that's a function of magnification and pixel pitch, not raw sensor area; see below).
- **Interchangeable, purpose-built lenses.** Unlike an iPhone's fixed, tiny, computational-photography-dependent lens system, Blackmagic bodies mount full manual-focus cinema or photo lenses (via MFT/EF/PL mounts), including genuinely purpose-built macro glass with none of the automatic multi-frame fusion smoothing described above — this is a real, meaningful quality difference, but it is a **glass and format difference, not a "locking" difference.**
- **Blackmagic RAW: 12-bit, uncompressed-at-the-sensor-level color pipeline**, storing raw sensor data (in a non-linear 12-bit space for maximum dynamic range) rather than passing it through anything resembling Deep Fusion's per-region neural denoising/fusion.[^blackmagic-raw] This is the actual analogue to the ProRAW recommendation above — a raw, minimally-processed capture path — not a "locked resolution/zoom" mechanism.
- **What "locked" actually buys a cinematographer**: consistent, repeatable exposure/focus/white-balance across a shot or a sequence of shots (so lighting and framing don't drift take to take) — the same *consistency* benefit AVFoundation's manual-control API offers CardVision above, not a resolution or detail unlock. **The user's premise is understandable (Blackmagic cameras are genuinely associated with "manual, locked-down" shooting) but the causal story is backwards: locking controls is something you can afford to do once you already have a bigger sensor, better glass, and a raw codec — it isn't what produces the image quality by itself.** A locked-down iPhone shooting through the same small lens and tiny sensor would still be bounded by the same optical ceiling from §6.6, ProRAW notwithstanding.

**Does "locking zoom" improve ground-sample-distance at all?** This needs to be split cleanly into two different things the user may mean:
- **Digital zoom: no, confirmed, adds zero real resolution.** Digital zoom is pure crop-and-upscale — it selects a sub-region of the existing pixel grid and interpolates it back up to display size; interpolation cannot reconstruct detail the optics and sensor never captured in the first place.[^digital-zoom] Locking or fixing a digital zoom level changes nothing about the underlying microns-per-pixel-at-the-subject; it is strictly a display/framing convenience, not a resolution technique. This should be stated plainly to avoid any ambiguity: **do not use digital zoom for CardVision capture, ever — it cannot help and can only reduce effective quality via interpolation and compression artifacts.**
- **Optical zoom (switching to the telephoto lens): plausible in principle, defeated in practice by minimum focus distance.** A longer physical focal length genuinely can reduce microns-per-pixel at the subject *if* the lens can also focus close enough to fill the frame with the small area of interest — magnification is a function of both focal length and how close the lens can focus, not focal length alone. This is exactly where the iPhone's telephoto lens fails for CardVision's purpose: the iPhone 15 Pro's telephoto is a **77mm-equivalent, ƒ/2.8 lens**,[^iphone-tele-spec] but telephoto lenses on iPhone are reported to need roughly **20–25cm minimum focus distance on earlier Pro models, and reportedly around 4 feet (~120cm) on the iPhone 15 Pro Max's 5x telephoto** — far beyond the **2cm** minimum focus distance of the ultrawide lens Apple's own Macro Mode uses.[^tele-min-focus] Get too close with the telephoto and the camera system **automatically falls back to a digital zoom crop of the main lens** instead of true telephoto optics, precisely because the telephoto physically cannot focus that close.[^tele-min-focus] **Net effect: the telephoto's longer focal length is real, but its far-longer minimum focus distance means it cannot be used to fill the frame with a small patch of card surface texture the way the ultrawide macro lens can — the magnification benefit is cancelled out by the inability to get close.** This is a case where a real optical capability (longer focal length) fails to translate into a practical GSD improvement for this specific object size, an important nuance the prior section's numbers didn't need to address but this direct question does. **Verdict: no, there is no "zoom trick" available in the iPhone's stock camera system — digital or optical — that beats the already-identified 5–30 micron estimate from the ultrawide macro lens; the only way past that ceiling remains the external clip-on macro lens path from §6.5.**

**Verdict for 6.8, direct and short.** Locked-focus/locked-exposure/locked-white-balance capture via AVFoundation is a real, cheap, worth-doing consistency improvement for CardVision's capture flow, and shooting ProRAW plus a custom minimal-smoothing raw-processing step is a genuine, previously-missed lever that should recover some real detail Apple's default Deep Fusion/Smart HDR pipeline is likely smoothing away as though it were sensor noise (inference, not directly tested on cards, but well-grounded in the documented mechanism). **Neither changes the optical resolution ceiling established in §6.6** — they're a software/pipeline fix stacked on top of an optics problem that's still there. Blackmagic's reputation comes from sensor size, interchangeable macro-capable glass, and a raw 12-bit codec, not from "locking resolution and zoom" — the user's causal story is backwards, though the raw-codec parallel (Blackmagic RAW ↔ Apple ProRAW) is a genuinely useful one to borrow. And there is no zoom trick, digital or optical, that gets an iPhone past the ultrawide macro lens's existing ~5–30 micron estimate — digital zoom adds nothing, and the telephoto's much longer minimum focus distance cancels out its longer focal length for an object this small. **Practical recommendation: add ProRAW + AVFoundation-locked capture to the Sell-wizard flow now (cheap, software-only, real incremental benefit); it does not replace the §6.5–6.7 macro-lens/photometric-stereo path as the only route to actually closing the resolution gap.**

### 9.4 eBay's actual PSA mechanics — confirmed, with real friction numbers

Public eBay seller documentation and independent reporting confirm the mechanics roughly as described in the user's premise, with useful specifics:

- **Exclusivity, and its own history of narrowing.** PSA is now eBay's **sole** authenticator for *both* raw and graded trading cards under Authenticity Guarantee — notably, this wasn't always true: CGC previously handled raw-card authentication for eBay while PSA handled graded-card authentication, and eBay **consolidated to PSA exclusively** at a later point.[^ebay-psa-exclusive] That consolidation-toward-one-authenticator pattern is itself evidence against the idea that a large marketplace keeps multiple authenticators active — eBay tried a split arrangement and moved away from it.
- **Threshold**: eligible items are $200+ singles (lowered from $250), and for graded cards, $2,000+ initially with a stated intent to expand down to $250+.[^ebay-psa-exclusive]
- **Flow**: seller ships to "the authentication facility shown in your order details" (an eBay/PSA-operated hub, not directly to a PSA public submission address) → PSA inspects (multi-point check for raw cards; case/label verification only, no regrade, for already-graded cards) → PSA reseals/sleeves the card with an authentication sticker and QR code → forwards to the buyer via tracked shipping.[^ebay-psa-flow] eBay, not the seller, pays for authentication.[^ebay-psa-flow]
- **Added turnaround, with real numbers**: eBay's own help documentation states authenticators "typically" inspect within 1–2 business days, plus "4-day secure delivery" to the buyer after passing inspection[^ebay-psa-flow] — call it **roughly a week of added transit/processing time over a direct seller-to-buyer shipment** in the documented best case. Independent reporting and eBay's own community forum tell a less rosy real-world story: value-added-resource.net reported **persistent authentication delays** with PSA pushing back its own processing timeframe commitments, and eBay sellers report **7+ day delays** are common during high-volume periods, with knock-on effects like **10–14 day payment holds** creating real working-capital pressure on sellers running tight cash flow.[^ebay-delays] **This is a materially larger and less predictable friction cost than the "1-2 day inspection" headline number suggests — worth flagging to the user directly, since it means even a hypothetical, perfectly-priced CardVision-adjacent physical-authentication partnership would add real, variable order friction, not just a cost line.**

### 9.5 Financial reality check

The user's own napkin math ($200 × 2% = $4 commission, eaten mostly by even CGC's $3/card cheapest rate) holds up and, if anything, is optimistic once the newly-found numbers are folded in:

- **Beckett's only public precedent price ($50/card, 2022, full-grade) is 12.5x AuctionHous's entire $4 commission on a $200 item** — not a rounding error, categorically unworkable at eBay's "platform eats the cost" model.
- **Even CGC's cheapest current rate ($3/card, authentication-only, conditional on sale within 30 days) consumes 75% of the commission on a $200 item**, exactly as the user calculated — and that's before shipping both directions, insurance on an in-transit high-value card, and any operational overhead of running the hub/routing logistics eBay operates (§9.4) in-house or via a partner.
- **The math gets worse, not better, at lower price points** — since eligibility thresholds in every real-world precedent (eBay's $200, CGC/Fanatics' flat per-card fee) are flat dollar amounts regardless of item value, a $200 card and a $2,000 card cost the authenticator the same $3–$9, but a $200 card's 2% commission is $4 while a $2,000 card's is $40 — meaning **this model, if it ever works, only works comfortably well above the $200 floor**, not at it.
- **Charging it as a buyer- or seller-paid add-on fee (rather than eating it like eBay does) changes the math meaningfully, and is worth taking seriously as the realistic structure** — eBay can afford to eat the cost because its take rate (~13.25% blended, per this project's own framing) leaves far more margin per transaction to absorb a $3–$9 authentication cost than AuctionHous's ~2% does. An explicit "physical authentication" opt-in fee, charged to whichever party wants the assurance (plausibly the buyer, who is the one protected against a swap on delivery, or split), decouples the authentication cost from the 2%-commission economics entirely and is architecturally closer to how CGC's own $3/$6/$9 tiers are already priced as an add-on service rather than baked into a platform-wide fee — this is the more financially survivable version of the idea, but it's a different product decision (an optional paid add-on) than "replicate eBay's Authenticity Guarantee," which is specifically a *platform-absorbed, zero-seller/buyer-cost* guarantee. Framing it to the user this way is more useful than a flat "no."

### 9.6 Verdict: not realistically available today, and Beckett is the wrong target even if it were

**Direct answer: no, a Beckett-style deal is not realistically available to AuctionHous today, for three independent reasons, any one of which would be sufficient on its own:**

1. **Beckett has no authentication-only product at any price, publicly, today** — its one marketplace-partnership precedent (2022, PWCC, $50/card) was full grading, not the fast/cheap authentication-only check the user is picturing, and it appears to have been supplanted by SGC's and later CGC's cheaper deals with the same marketplace partner rather than continued or expanded.
2. **Beckett is being absorbed into PSA's own corporate parent (Collectors)** — pursuing Beckett specifically *because* it's "not PSA" stops making strategic sense once the acquisition closes; CGC is now the more relevant independent alternative to research if AuctionHous wants a real PSA-family alternative.
3. **Every real precedent found (CGC/Fanatics, SGC/PWCC, Beckett/PWCC) is a large-marketplace-to-grader deal, not something graders publish a self-serve rate card for** — none of the public announcements disclose volume commitments or exclusivity terms explicitly, but the pattern (a handful of deals total, each with a marketplace processing tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of cards — recall CGC's own reporting of 570,000 cards graded in a single record month) strongly implies these are negotiated, relationship-and-volume-gated arrangements, not a menu item a pre-revenue or early-stage marketplace can simply order off of. No public source confirms an explicit minimum-volume threshold (this is inference, not a cited fact), but the sheer scale of the counterparties in every disclosed deal is itself evidence of the gate.

**Concrete sequencing recommendation**: don't pursue a physical-authentication partnership now. The realistic path is (a) build real submission/order volume using CardVision's free, always-on optical-matching layer as the trust mechanism for the below-$200-threshold long tail, where physical authentication was never going to be economical anyway (§9.5's math); (b) once AuctionHous has a demonstrable, countable stream of above-$200 high-value transactions — the number a grader would actually size a deal against — approach **CGC**, not Beckett, both because CGC is the one player with recent, disclosed precedent for exactly this authentication-only-tier product (the $3/$6/$9 Fanatics Collect structure is a template AuctionHous could point to directly in a pitch) and because CGC remains outside the PSA/Collectors corporate family, preserving the "genuine alternative to PSA" positioning the user is actually after; and (c) price it as an **optional, buyer/seller-paid add-on fee**, not a platform-absorbed cost like eBay's, since §9.5's math shows AuctionHous's 2% take rate cannot absorb even CGC's cheapest current rate the way eBay's much larger blended take rate can.
